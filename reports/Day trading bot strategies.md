# Replace six voting bots with tested sleeves

The best-supported design for this bot is not more voters. It is one or two fully specified, independently tracked strategies: a relative-volume-gated opening-range breakout held to the close, and possibly a SPY/QQQ "noise band" intraday-momentum sleeve. Each runs with its own risk budget, and costs are modelled honestly. The 3-of-6 vote should be retired. The repo's own backtests show why it has to go: the vote behaves like a single signal, since the trend bot sided with **97–100% of all backtested trades**, and its gross edge of **0.02–0.07R per trade** is smaller than its modelled cost of **0.10–0.14R per trade**. Every backtest year (2024, 2025, 2025 with the SPY gate, and 2026) is positive on perfect paper fills and negative after estimated slippage, and every one trails SPY buy-and-hold. The live record of 21 trades is too small to judge any strategy. It is still consistent with that picture: 11 stop-outs, trailing-stop exits averaging +$9 gross against about $12 of modelled cost, and only about 2 net-of-cost wins. Even the strategies that should replace the vote carry poor odds. Most human day traders lose money. The best-documented intraday edges come mainly from one research group, and they shrank to roughly zero or below once independent researchers added realistic fills and tested the years after publication. The realistic goal for v2 is to find out cheaply and rigorously whether *any* sleeve has an edge, not to tune the current one into profitability. Confirming a modest edge takes hundreds of trades per sleeve, which means months of frozen paper trading, not weeks.

*This is a summary of research evidence and engineering options. It is not financial advice, and the author is not a financial advisor.*

## Day trading starts in a hole, and the best published edges have been shrinking

The base rates are bleak. In complete Taiwan exchange records from 1992 to 2006, only about **13–15% of day traders were profitable net of costs in a typical year**, and **fewer than 1% (about 1,000 of 360,000)** were predictably profitable year after year ([Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/day%20traders/Day%20Trading%20Skill%20110523.pdf)). A follow-up estimates that **97% of day traders are likely to lose money** in future day trading ([Barber et al., RAPS 2020](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rasset/v10y2020i1p61-93..html)). The Brazilian study is the hardest result for a bot builder to dismiss, because mini-index futures are cheap to trade. Among Brazilians who day-traded those futures for 300 or more days, **97% lost money and 0.4% earned more than a bank teller's starting wage** ([Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti](https://ideas.repec.org/p/fgv/eesptd/525.html)). A bot removes some behavioural mistakes, but none of these studies suggest automation supplies an edge against faster, better-informed counterparties.

Only two intraday families show large positive net results in well-documented work, and both come from Zarattini, Aziz and Barbon (Concretum). The first is a **5-minute opening-range breakout restricted to "stocks in play."** Each day it trades the top 20 US stocks by first-5-minute relative volume (RelVol ≥ 100% of the 14-day average for that window), enters on a stop at the first candle's extreme in that candle's direction, uses a stop at 10% of the 14-day ATR, sets no target, and exits at 16:00. From 2016 to 2023 it reported a **Sharpe of 2.81 and a 48.4% hit rate**, net of commissions but with no explicit slippage ([paper PDF](https://alexandria.unisg.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/3c2989c4-688d-4d78-8a71-f02690990d51/content)). The second is **SPY "Beat the Market" noise-area momentum.** It goes long or short when price closes beyond a time-of-day band built from the 14-day average move from the open *and* beyond VWAP, checks only on the half-hour, trails at max(band, VWAP), and flattens at the close. It reported **19.6% a year and a Sharpe of 1.33 from 2007 to 2024**, with a trade hit rate of 37% ([Beat the Market PDF](https://alexandria.unisg.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/a99aba00-f967-49b3-aceb-f544dc386e0b/content)).

Independent out-of-sample work in 2026 cuts both results down sharply. A frozen-parameter replication of Beat the Market fell to **4.8% annualized and a reported Sharpe of 0.39 for May 2024 to March 2026**, behind SPY buy-and-hold ([PazSheimy OOS repo](https://github.com/PazSheimy/spy-intraday-momentum-oos); [Swedroe](https://larryswedroe.substack.com/p/when-beat-the-market-meets-the-real)). That Sharpe figure is internally loose, since 4.8% return on 14.7% volatility works out nearer 0.33. A pre-registered test of the stocks-in-play ORB charged a 1.5 bp half-spread plus adverse selection of 0.025×ATR on stop entries. Its gross edge fell from **+0.206R per trade (2016–19) to +0.078R (2020–June 2026)**, and its net result was **−0.0855R per trade (t = −2.16), Sharpe −0.69** ([Pricing-the-Fill](https://github.com/js3872/Pricing-the-Fill)). Replications of the QQQ ORB reproduce the gross edge (+0.131R on NQ), but net of spread and slippage no index is distinguishable from zero ([MQL5 replication](https://www.mql5.com/en/blogs/post/776235)). The academic market-intraday-momentum effect (first half-hour predicts last half-hour) had a slope of 0.003 (t = 0.20) from 2013 to 2019, so it was already flat before publication ([Temperance Capital](https://temperancecapital.substack.com/p/market-intraday-momentum-replicates)). These replications are rigorous practitioner work, not peer-reviewed papers, but they agree with one another. For a faithful, cost-aware build, a fair prior is a Sharpe between zero and about 0.5, with results heavily dependent on the volatility regime.

Two findings matter most for v2's fixed list of 30. First, the ORB paper's edge comes almost entirely from **daily selection**. The identical rule run on all eligible stocks earned a **Sharpe of 0.48, worse than buy-and-hold**, and trades with RelVol under 100% averaged **−0.02R**, compared with +0.08R above 100% and +0.38R above 30x ([paper PDF](https://alexandria.unisg.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/3c2989c4-688d-4d78-8a71-f02690990d51/content)). A fixed large-cap watchlist traded every day sits in that low-edge bucket. Second, intraday mean reversion has no documented net edge for large caps. Short-term reversal earns **+0.93% a month overnight and −1.05% a month intraday** ([Lou, Polk & Skouras](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/polk/research/TugOfWar.pdf)), and short-lag intraday reversals are mostly bid-ask bounce, which loses money once you pay the spread ([Heston, Korajczyk & Sadka](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1005.3535)). A flat-by-close meanrev bot is betting against the documented direction.

## Six voters act like one, and adding four more would not change that

Combining forecasts helps only when the inputs carry *different* information. The forecast-combination literature finds that the benefit of averaging "shrinks as the underlying information sets become more correlated," and that redundant copies of a forecast actively degrade a simple average ([Qian et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1505.00475)). v2's voters are vwap, trend, momentum and ORB, which are all functions of the same recent 1-minute price path, plus relvol, which is a volume condition rather than a direction, and meanrev, which by design fires against the others. The standard effective-count formula N_eff = N / (1 + (N−1)ρ) shows the problem. At an average pairwise correlation of 0.6, six voters are worth **about 1.5 independent signals**, and ten would be worth **about 1.56**. A 10-bot committee built from the same bars adds latency and parameters, not breadth.

The repo's backtests confirm this directly. In the 2025 run of 10,223 trades, the trend bot voted with **9,938 trades (97%)**, vwap with 9,733 and relvol with 9,442. Meanrev voted with **19 trades in the whole year**, at a 26% win rate, and ORB with 2,609 (`data/backtest_v2/REPORT.md`). In the 2026 run, trend voted with **1,178 of 1,178 trades** (`data/backtest_2026/REPORT.md`). The "≥3 agree" rule therefore reduces to "price is trending on the 1-minute EMAs, is on the right side of VWAP, and volume is up," and the extra agreement mostly adds lateness. ORB-voted trades did lose less than the rest in every year: −0.036R to −0.055R net per trade, against −0.075R to −0.108R without ORB, computed from the repo's trade files. That is the one internal hint that the breakout component carries something the others don't. Still, v2's ORB is a 15-minute range with breakouts allowed until minute 120 (`v2/bots.py`), not the 5-minute candle-direction rule the evidence supports.

The per-bot scoreboard cannot carry the weight being put on it. The roughly 10% hit rate counts wins *after modelled cost*. Gross, 6 of 21 live trades won, about 29%. Net, about 2 of 21 did. For 2 wins in 20 trades, the 95% interval on the true hit rate runs from about **1.2% to 31.7%**, so the data already reject "hit rate ≥ 40%" (probability about 0.36%) but cannot tell a 15% strategy from a 25% one. Adaptive reweighting on samples this small chases noise. Equal weights repeatedly beat estimated or adaptive weights out of sample because estimation error swamps the theoretical gain ([Qian et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1505.00475)). Persistent skill among day traders shows up only when measured over a full prior year of data ([Barber et al.](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/day%20traders/Day%20Trading%20Skill%20110523.pdf)). v2's `MIN_VOTES_FOR_WEIGHT = 30` is far too few trades to justify moving weights.

"Many bots" makes sense only as **separate sleeves**: different strategies, each with its own capital, risk budget and trade log, so you can see which one is losing ([Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/should-investors-combine-or-separate-their-factor-exposures/)). Diversifying across sleeves raises portfolio Sharpe only if each sleeve has positive net expectancy on its own. Averaging zero-edge components produces zero edge with smaller swings, which makes losses look smaller without removing them.

## Costs of 0.10–0.23R per trade swamp a gross edge of 0.02–0.07R

In v2's backtests, a typical stop-loss costs about $43–59, which approximates 1R. On that scale the gross edge was **+0.065R per trade in 2024, +0.044R in 2025 with the gate, and +0.018R in 2026**, while the 3 bp/side cost model charged **0.10–0.14R per trade**. Net expectancy was −0.06R to −0.08R in every year. Costs ran to **213–566% of gross P&L** (`data/backtest_2024/REPORT.md`, `data/backtest_2026/REPORT.md`). Live trades were worse. The average modelled cost was **$11.61 against an average stop-loss of −$50.28, or 0.23R per trade**, and the 21 trades total −$713 net of modelled cost (`data/v2/trades.csv`). For comparison, the *entire* documented gross edge of the best ORB variants is about 0.1–0.2R, and the Pricing-the-Fill author found the strategy broke even only when adverse selection was about 0.008×ATR out of sample, which amounts to "essentially free fills" ([Pricing-the-Fill](https://github.com/js3872/Pricing-the-Fill)). The config's `MAX_COST_TO_TARGET = 0.20` lets a single trade's cost equal that whole edge.

The exit design pushes the payoff ratio in the wrong direction. v2 takes profit at 1R, moves the stop to breakeven once a trade reaches 0.4R, and closes trades that "go nowhere" after 60 minutes (`v2/config.py`, `v2/exits.py`). The result is roughly symmetric wins and losses: an average win of $32 against an average loss of $36 in 2025. With full −1R losses, the breakeven win rate is p* = (1+c)/(W+1). At W ≈ 0.9R and c ≈ 0.1R that works out to **about 58%**, while the paper win rate was 55% before costs and 45% after. Every documented profitable intraday system takes the opposite shape. They have **37–48% hit rates, no fixed target, and hold to the close or trail a structural level checked every 30 minutes**, because a minority of large winners pays for everything ([Beat the Market PDF](https://alexandria.unisg.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/a99aba00-f967-49b3-aceb-f544dc386e0b/content); [ORB paper](https://alexandria.unisg.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/3c2989c4-688d-4d78-8a71-f02690990d51/content)). Only 2–4% of QQQ ORB trades ever reach 10R, yet those trades carry the gross edge ([MQL5 replication](https://www.mql5.com/en/blogs/post/776235)). An early breakeven lock cuts off exactly those trades. Kaminski and Lo add a theoretical point: stops raise expected return only when returns are positively autocorrelated, so tight stops on mean-reversion entries are structurally negative ([Kaminski & Lo](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/114876/Lo_When%20Do%20Stop-Loss.pdf)). The fast stop-outs fit opening noise as well. Volatility decays roughly as a power law over the first two hours ([Allez & Bouchaud](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1009.4785)), and **31–45% of backtested stop-outs came within 10 minutes**, with a median hold of 12 minutes on live 11-23 trades.

The data and fill assumptions also flatter the results. Free Alpaca data is IEX only, about **3.9% of US share volume** in February 2026 ([SIFMA](https://www.sifma.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/SIFMA-Insights-Equities-and-Options-Monthly-Metrics_February_2026.pdf)), which is higher than the 2.5% the repo's reports assume. Bar volumes and highs and lows are therefore thin samples, and the signals that depend on them (RelVol, VWAP, range extremes) are the most distorted. A RelVol *ratio* computed IEX-to-IEX should partly cancel this, but that is an inference, not a measurement. Paper fills happen at the NBBO with no size check, no latency slippage and no market impact ([Alpaca paper docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/paper-trading)), so paper P&L sets an upper bound on live P&L. Scheduling has become the binding operational failure. GitHub documents that scheduled workflows can be delayed under load, and one 2026 report describes runs **8–14.5 hours late on six consecutive days, with two that never fired** ([GitHub Community #201738](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/201738)). That matches 11-25, when the job started after the close. v2 does keep its protective stop at Alpaca as an OTO stop order (`v2/broker.py`), so a late or dead job cannot leave a position unprotected. But it can miss entire sessions, and if the job dies mid-day, nothing flattens open positions before the close.

Two other items in the brief are less important than they look. The Gemini news filter already fails open: `run_news_desk` catches the `ModuleNotFoundError` from the deleted v1 `news_filter` and, after four tries, trades without news (`v2/bots.py`, `v2/engine.py`). The evidence also says little is lost. LLM headline signals decayed from a Sharpe of 6.54 in 2021Q4 to 1.22 by early 2024, become unprofitable at about 20 bps round-trip, and work mainly in small caps and on bad news ([Lopez-Lira & Tang](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.07619v6)). Earnings press releases are fully priced at the open, and a long-short entered at 9:45 earned negative alpha ([Wu et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.24254v2)). News is best used as a cheap veto, or as the reason a stock shows high RelVol, not as a direction signal. The SPY regime gate was derived from the 2024 and 2026 data (per the comment in `v2/config.py`). On 2025 it cut trades from 10,223 to 865 and improved expectancy from −$5 to −$3 per trade. Net expectancy stayed negative, and no rigorous source shows that a simple SPY-trend gate improves intraday single-stock strategies.

One more problem undermines every number above: **the trade log does not reconcile with the account**. On 11-23, the gross P&L in `trades.csv` sums to −$284, while `daily.csv` records an equity change of **+$236**. On 11-24 the figures are −$185 and −$381. Until the log is rebuilt from Alpaca's actual fills, neither the scoreboard nor the reported daily P&L can be trusted.

## Count trades per sleeve, not calendar weeks

Validation should run in two stages, with the spec frozen before each one. In the backtest stage, run each sleeve on 2024, 2025 and 2026 IEX bars under the pessimistic fill model from change 11. Advance a sleeve only if net expectancy is positive in each year separately, survives doubling the cost assumption, and beats the random-direction control. Count every variant tried in the trial register. All three years have already informed earlier v2 settings, so treat them as semi-in-sample. Forward paper trading is the only clean holdout.

In the paper stage, the arithmetic sets the timeline. Reaching t = 2 takes n = (2/SR)² trades, where SR is the per-trade Sharpe (mean P&L over its standard deviation). That is **about 100 trades at a per-trade Sharpe of 0.2 and about 400 at 0.1**, and the documented intraday edges sit at the low end of that range. Detecting a 5-point hit-rate edge takes about 385 trades. Bailey and López de Prado's minimum track record length implies **roughly 2–3 years of daily returns** before even a single-trial Sharpe near 1 is distinguishable from zero at 95% confidence ([Deflated Sharpe ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflated_Sharpe_ratio)).

The checkpoints below are kill-or-continue gates, not proof. Any parameter change restarts the count.

| Checkpoint (per sleeve) | Rough time (Sleeve A at 2–4 trades/day; Sleeve B at about 1/day) | Decision rule |
|---|---|---|
| 50 trades | A: 3–5 weeks; B: about 2.5 months | Stop if net expectancy ≤ −0.1R, or if it falls outside the backtest's 5th–95th percentile band |
| 100 trades | A: 1.5–2.5 months; B: about 5 months | Continue only if net expectancy > 0 and gross metrics (hit rate, average win/loss in R, MAE/MFE) match the backtest |
| 200 trades | A: 3–5 months; B: about 10 months | Compute the deflated Sharpe using the trial register; compare with SPY buy-and-hold and the random control |
| 400 trades | A: 5–10 months; B: about 1.5–2 years | Minimum before considering small live capital, and then expect live costs to exceed paper costs |

## Conclusion

The current v2 problem is not that the bots vote badly. It is that the whole design (fixed universe, tight stops, a 1R target, an early breakeven lock) sits in the part of strategy space that the evidence marks as negative after costs, and that adding voters cannot fix that. The useful reframe is that v2 is a measurement instrument. Its job is to tell, cheaply and without fooling Dylan, whether a stocks-in-play breakout or a SPY noise-band sleeve keeps any edge after realistic fills on IEX data. That shifts the priorities toward reconciled fill logs, reliable session starts, pessimistic backtests and a frozen spec with trade-count checkpoints, and away from more signals, gates or model calls. The honest base case is that both sleeves come out near zero net. A clean negative result after 100–200 trades per sleeve would still be a success for the project. The benchmark the bot has to beat is SPY buy-and-hold, which returned +12% to +25% in every backtest window, while every after-cost v2 result was negative.
